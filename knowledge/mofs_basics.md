# Metal-organic frameworks: basics

## What a MOF is
Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are crystalline porous materials built from metal ions or metal-oxo clusters (nodes) connected by organic molecules (linkers) into extended 2D or 3D networks. Changing the metal, the linker or the network topology changes pore size, pore shape and surface chemistry. This tunability is why MOFs are studied for gas storage (H2, CH4), CO2 capture, gas separation, catalysis, sensing and drug delivery.

## Why surface area and porosity matter
Adsorption happens on the internal pore walls, so more accessible surface area and more void volume generally mean more room for guest molecules. Some MOFs reach accessible surface areas above 6,000 m²/g; in this CoRE-MOF table the maximum is 8,318 m²/g.

## Open metal sites
An open metal site (OMS) is a metal ion in the framework with a free coordination position, typically created by removing a coordinated solvent molecule during activation. Open metal sites bind polarisable or quadrupolar molecules such as CO2 strongly and can act as Lewis-acid catalytic centres. They also bind water, which can reduce performance or stability in humid conditions. In this table, 66 % of MOFs are flagged as having an open metal site.

## Well-known MOFs and their CSD refcodes
- HKUST-1 (Cu-BTC, MOF-199): FIQCEN (copper paddle-wheel with open Cu sites)
- ZIF-8: OFERUN (zinc imidazolate framework)
- UiO-66: RUBTAK family (zirconium framework known for high stability)
- MOF-5 (IRMOF-1): SAHYIK, which is not in this CoRE-MOF table

## Computational screening
High-throughput screening ranks thousands of real or hypothetical MOFs with fast geometric descriptors or simulations before any synthesis. An example is Wilmer et al., "Large-scale screening of hypothetical metal–organic frameworks", Nature Chem. 4, 83–89 (2012). Geometric screening (as in MOFChat) is the cheapest first filter. Grand-canonical Monte Carlo (GCMC) simulation and experiments are needed to confirm adsorption performance.
